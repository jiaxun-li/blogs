For the first time, *Pentiment* made the word “history” feel alive with emotion. She is the ship of Theseus, endlessly taken apart and rebuilt, but more passionate; she is Jesus, endlessly dying and rising again, but more bewildered. She's whirling in rags, she's vast and she's sad.

*Pentiment* feels more like a work of literature than a game: its gameplay leaves much to be desired, but its portrayal of people and their world is faultless. The feeling it gave me was unlike anything else. You are a painter in a small Bavarian town in the early sixteenth century. Martin Luther, the printing press, the German Peasants’ War, the Renaissance—these cold phrases from textbooks become forces that actually shape your life and the lives of those around you. The wheel of history rolls forward, and you are an ant on its hub.

The revelation at the end is staggering. When the mystery finally unraveled, it felt as though more than a thousand years came pouring down at once. I saw the ancestors of Tassing sitting around a fire, worshipping their gods. I saw Romans mining ore and building baths. I saw the abbey slowly taking shape, and Christianity gradually spreading through the town. And I saw a blank canvas: the canvas that, after everything the people of Tassing had been through, was still waiting to be painted.

As I played, I found myself almost envying the people of Tassing. They live on layers of history, among Roman ruins, under the protection of saints, surrounded by ancient legends. It is as though they live alongside their ancestors. Where I live, there’s nothing. I have no idea what the Native Americans or the Germanic invaders left behind on this land. Perhaps just road construction and long winters. Thankfully, technology has given me books, games, and films to keep me company. There may be no Roman ruins next to my house, but there are some inside the little glowing box in front of me. That’s pretty good, too.

The characters live at a moment when an old world is giving way to a new one, across a boundary historians would draw between the Middle Ages and the modern era. For better and for worse, they were born into this age of change. The prosperity of the past goes up in flames, while the future follows a rough road toward something brighter.

And here I am, living in the age of AI. A few years ago, it was the pandemic; a few years before that, China’s economic boom. I don’t know whether future historians will also draw a dividing line through these years. I don’t know where time’s endlessly swirling skirt will fling me. But I hope I can face it with the same openness as Brother Piero:

> “Time passes for us all, things change, the future will write over the present. You need not fear it, any of it, so long as you remain true to yourself and God.”
