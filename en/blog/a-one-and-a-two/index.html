A one and a two

Sep 25, 2025·
Jiaxun Li
· 4 min read
A one and a two

I finished watching Yi Yi at around 10 p.m. After walking a friend to the bus stop, I went back to West Hall to grade some papers. I couldn’t help but think back to this afternoon’s office hours, when I had an argument with a student over points. It wasn’t even my fault, yet I couldn’t win the argument, and I began reflecting on why I’m so weak. Then I thought of similar situations I’d faced before in student clubs—moments where I had also reflected on this same weakness. Life moves in circles: things may seem to change, but in reality they just reappear in different forms. That’s also how I felt after watching Yi Yi.

I had been afraid that a three-hour film would feel too long, but while watching, I actually wished it would last even longer. Three hours are not enough to capture a whole life, but just enough to portray a family’s one year. From a wedding to a funeral, too much happens in Nanjun’s family in the span of a year: a major company project, a trip to Tokyo, A-di’s marriage, a new baby, Ting-ting’s first love, and the grandmother’s death. Though the film narrates all of this with an extremely calm lens and tone, it still can’t conceal the turbulence in the characters’ hearts. Every member of Nanjun’s family, from old to young, shares the same trait, which a friend of mine described as “seemingly calm, but actually pretty unsteady”: NJ may look like the honest man, but he acts against profit-driven motives and often opposes his fellow executives; Ting-ting, the obedient top student, blames herself for her grandmother’s coma and loses sleep over it; Yang-yang doesn’t like to talk, but his mind is always filled with strange ideas. The character portrayals in Yi Yi are so delicate that each of the dozen or so major characters feels vivid and full. Even a seemingly ordinary side character—after a few appearances—suddenly turns out to be a murderer, and yet it doesn’t feel out of place.

I really like what Fatty says: “Since the invention of cinema, human life has been lengthened by at least three times.” Sometimes I feel like I have all sorts of messy thoughts that would sound childish if spoken aloud. I’m not even sure whether they’re real thoughts or just the so-called “restlessness of youth,” so I keep them hidden. But after watching Yi Yi, I felt that everyone is like this. Nanjun, A-di, Lili—they all appear calm but are full of hidden worries, sometimes doing reckless things. A few days ago I had finished Park Chan-wook’s Vengeance Trilogy. If Sympathy for Mr. Vengeancecompresses anger until it explodes, tearing everything apart to show what humans really are, then Yi Yi is the beating heart beneath a calm mask: look, even though it’s just everyday family matters, people are like this too.

If you ask me which character I liked most, I’d answer without hesitation: Yang-yang. It even feels like the director couldn’t hold back his affection for him: the film ends with him, and the poster features him as well (PS: many people say Yang-yang is Yang Yang). In his eyes there is not only innocence and curiosity, but also a kind of firmness—something absent from the softened gazes of the weathered adults. Even when bullied by girls and teachers, he stubbornly raises his camera to photograph the backs of people’s heads, showing them what they cannot see for themselves. He says, “Grandma, there are so many things I don’t know. Do you know what I want to do in the future? I want to tell people what they don’t know, show them what they can’t see. I think that way, every day will be fun.” At the wedding, the grandmother—who knows and has experienced everything—chooses silence. At the funeral, the child—who knows nothing and has experienced nothing—speaks at length, wanting to know more. Life is a cycle. If possible, I’d want to always remain Yang-yang.

By the time I finish writing this, it’s already 2 a.m. Tomorrow I’ll have office hours again—hopefully calmer than today. After watching the film, I still can’t see the back of my own head, but at least I’ve seen others’. It feels like I know a little more, and also realize a little more of what I don’t know. The film is called Yi Yi—perhaps that’s exactly what it means.
